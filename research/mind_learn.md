# The Mind of a Boxer: how fighters learn, and how the levels differ

For the skill-level system (`docs/BRAIN.md` "Skill levels", L0 to L6) and learning across fights. Doesn't repeat `mind_vision.md` (gaze, anticipation, reaction times, feints, composure, fatigue): it points there instead.

**Tags:** [source] = page read; [snippet] = search-result text only (the proxy blocked most boxing sites); [mem] = from memory, verify; (est.) = my estimate for the engine. Studies are small (often 7 to 30 boxers): treat every number as a direction, not a law.

## 1. Novice vs pro: the tells

### 1a. What novices do wrong (and what it costs)

| Tell | What it looks like | What it costs | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Telegraphing | Wind-up, shoulder dip, hand pulled back before it goes | Opponent reads it early and counters | [snippet] evolve-university, expertboxing |
| Arm punching | Punch thrown with the arm, no hip or trunk turn | Much less force; novices twist the upper body instead of turning the whole trunk | [snippet] trunk EMG study (8 exp vs 7 novice) |
| Squaring up | Chest faces the opponent | Bigger target, weaker rear hand, easy to walk through | [snippet] coaching blogs |
| Crossing / lining up feet | One foot behind the other | Off balance on any head movement, rear hand loses its base | [snippet] coachweb (trainer quote) |
| Holding breath | No exhale on the punch | Weaker punches, gasses early | [snippet] several coaching blogs |
| Dropping the hands after a punch | Hand comes back low or late | Counter lands on the way back | [snippet] |
| Eyes closed / looking away or down | Flinch on incoming punches | Can't avoid, block or counter; unseen punches do more damage | [snippet] evolve-mma, expertboxing first-spar checklist |
| Leaning back / straight back | Pulls straight back from everything | Runs out of room, no counter position | [snippet] zebra athletics (counter drills fix it) |
| Freezing | Locks up after a hard shot or under pressure | Eats the follow-ups | [snippet] mind_vision 6 |
| Looping punches | Wide arcs on hooks and rights | Slower, shorter one beats it | [mem] trainer lore |
| One punch at a time | Throws, stops, looks | Nothing to set up, easy to time | [snippet] novice study: winners threw more combos |
| Gassing | Tense shoulders, over-commits, holds breath | Round 3 output collapses | [snippet] + (est.) |
| Fixed gaze on the wrong thing | Watches the gloves, high search rate | Late reads; see `mind_vision.md` 1 | [snippet] Ripoll 1995 via review |

### 1b. The order pros fix them (the teaching ladder)

Coaching curricula disagree on details but agree on the spine [snippet: gymdesk 8-week plan, Leuphana course, Evolve fundamentals]:

1. **Stance + guard** (feet staggered, chin down, hands up). Fixes squaring up and crossed feet.
2. **Footwork basics** (step-drag, never cross). Some plans put it week 1, others week 4.
3. **Jab**, then the **cross** (1-2), with the exhale.
4. **Hook**, then uppercut, then **body** shots.
5. **Defense**: block/parry first, then slip, then slip-and-counter, roll later.
6. **Combinations** (1-2-3-2 by week 7 in one plan).
7. **Controlled sparring** (week 8 at the earliest). Eyes-open drills (glove taps, slow punches at the face) before and during.

Then, over years (from trainer lore and the cases below [mem]): punching with the legs, sitting down on the right hand, combos that change level, counters, feints, cutting the ring, reading habits, pacing, in-fight adjusting. **Fight IQ is the last layer.** A novice can have a good jab long before he can read a man.

Engine meaning: skills unlock in that order. A Novice's offense list is jab and 1-2; hooks loop, uppercuts and body work are rare and ugly. Feints, counters by defense, cutting off and adjusting are near zero below Intermediate.

### 1c. Expert vs novice studies

**Punch force (Smith et al. 2000, J Sports Sci; 7 elite, 8 intermediate, 8 novice)** [snippet, abstract]:

| Level | Rear straight | Lead straight | Rear vs elite |
|---|---|---|---|
| Novice | 2381 N | 1604 N | 0.50x |
| Intermediate | 3722 N | 2283 N | 0.78x |
| Elite | 4800 N | 2847 N | 1.00x |

Every gap is significant. **The big jump is novice to intermediate (+56% on the rear hand), then +29% to elite.** Lead hand gains less (0.56 → 0.80 → 1.0).

**Speed vs force:** a wearable study (31 boxers) found experts differ in punch **trajectory and force**, but **not velocity** [source: PMC8659887 via snippet]. Skill adds power through mechanics (legs, trunk, timing), not hand speed. Older work: amateurs' force came mostly from trunk rotation, pros' more from leg drive [snippet, 1985 study cited].

**Activity (amateurs)** [snippet: Davis et al.; Frontiers 2020 review]:
- Novice bouts (32 boxers, 3x2 min): winners landed **18 ± 11 more punches**, mainly more lead straights in round 1 and more combos in rounds 1 and 3.
- Elite male amateurs: ~1.55 actions/s, ~21 punches, ~3.6 defensive moves, ~56 bobs/dips per minute. Elite women ~16 punches, ~3.3 defensive moves per minute.
- Review: triple combos, block-and-counter combos and defensive effectiveness separate winners at both levels; **elites show higher frequencies of technical movements than novices.**

**Perception:** see `mind_vision.md` 2 and 3 (same simple reaction time, faster choice reaction, fewer fixations, the chest as a "visual pivot"). One add: in karate, novices fixate the pelvis and front hand with a **high search rate**; experts hold head/torso with a low one [snippet, Milazzo 2016]. In a 2022 combat-sport review, higher-level athletes had faster **and** more accurate responses [snippet].

**Composure by experience:** no boxing study found. Military parachute jumps: novices show higher heart rate, lactate and anxiety than experts, while fine motor skill stays the same [snippet]. Esports experts had *higher* pre-match cortisol (they care more). Read: experience lowers panic, not caring (est.).

## 2. How fighters learn across fights

### 2a. Case files: what changed after a loss (or a hard first fight)

| Fighter | Lesson event | What changed next time | Result | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wladimir Klitschko | KO'd by Brewster (2004), Sanders (2003) | Steward: measured, long heavy jab ("half jab" per Brewster), clinch to smother (vs Peter 2005), less reckless, steadier nerves | 11-year reign; stopped Brewster in the 2007 rematch | [snippet] boxingscene, Sky, boxingonly |
| Canelo | Lost to Mayweather (2013), a fight his corner made no adjustment in | Waist movement, tighter guard, lean-back counters, move only when needed (saves gas late), more patience; showed vs GGG and Jacobs | Became the era's best middle/super-middle | [snippet] DAZN, WBC, Round by Round |
| Pacquiao | KO'd by Marquez 4 (2012) | "No longer careless": more cautious, kept ferocity; critics still saw a dropped guard in kamikaze mode | Never stopped again, fewer KOs after | [snippet] Inquirer (Buboy Fernandez) |
| Marquez (the learner) | 41 rounds vs Pacquiao, 0-2-1 | Four months drilling one counter right on Manny stepping in; hid the right hand's path, switched straight → body hook → left | KO6 in fight 4 | [snippet] Philstar, ESPN interview |
| Joshua | Stopped by Ruiz (2019) | Jab at range, pivot out, feints to freeze Ruiz, clinch when close, no brawling | UD in the rematch 6 months later | [snippet] Frontproof, Reuters-based |
| Joshua | Lost to Usyk 1 (2021) | More body work, more aggression (new trainer) | Lost again, split; best moments were body attacks in R9 | [snippet] SI, CBS, BoxRaw |
| Usyk (the adjuster) | Joshua's body attack in fight 2 | From R4: circled right away from the right hand, combos to touch not hurt; after R9 tied him up inside; took R10 | Won both | [snippet] Sky, Bloody Elbow |
| Fury | Wilder 1 (2018): dropped twice, draw | Switched to SugarHill Steward; "seek and destroy": forward, body, heavy jab, leaned on him inside | TKO7 in the rematch | [snippet] Telegraph, Scroll |
| Mayweather | Castillo 1 (2002, hurt shoulder) | "Box more, no power shots, be smart": jab and right leads on the retreat; jab outlanded 88-36 | Closer card, still a win | [snippet] boxingnewsonline, wiki |
| Lomachenko | Lost to Salido in pro fight 2 (2014) | Learned the pro game: roughing inside, hips, low blows, weight; "a very good impression of what is a professional boxer" | Three-division champ | [snippet] Yahoo, ESPN, Ring |
| Bivol | Lost to Beterbiev 1 (2024): "I had to act, not just waiting" | Started earlier, more active | Won the rematch (MD, 2025) | [snippet] ESPN (see MIND.md) |
| Wilder (the non-learner) | Lost to Fury 2 | Fury 3: "one plan, knock him out, no plan B"; vs Parker 2023 "couldn't pull the trigger" | Lost both; "one-dimensional" | [snippet] Sowetan, Defector, SI |
| Fury (limit of learning) | Lost to Usyk 1 (2024) | Rematch plan changed, Usyk still better | Lost the rematch | [snippet] CNN/KTVZ |

**Patterns:**
1. **The fix is almost always one or two big changes, not ten.** Jab + clinch (Wlad), defense + patience (Canelo), range + pivots (AJ), pressure (Fury). (est. from cases)
2. **The usual fix after a KO loss is "more careful"**: less reckless, higher guard, fewer risks (Wlad, Pacquiao, AJ). Sometimes the change is the reverse: Fury went *more* aggressive because Wilder's power made running risky.
3. **A new trainer is often the trigger** (Steward for Wlad, SugarHill for Fury, Robert Garcia for AJ). Lessons need a teacher; the fighter alone mostly repeats himself. This is Last Bell's corner.
4. **Learning an opponent is specific and slow:** Marquez needed 3 fights + 4 months on one punch. A rematch lesson is about *that* man.
5. **Better skill still wins rematches.** Usyk beat Fury and AJ twice. A plan can't cover a whole skill tier.

### 2b. Rematch odds (how much a lesson is worth)

- Boxing, weakly sourced: the first-fight winner wins the rematch ~86 to 90% of the time [snippet, Queensberry Rules 2009, anecdotal].
- UFC title rematches: prior winner 63 to 69% [snippet, AgentMMA, UFCStats].
- Warnick & Warnick (2009, 739 boxers, BoxRec): **prior result vs the same opponent** and the last bout's result predict the next one. In the 2007 sample (400 bouts), only age, career wins/losses and the last bout mattered [snippet].
- Take: the loser's lesson closes some of the gap, not all of it. A rematch with no other change should go to the first winner ~70 to 80% (est.), unless the first fight was close or a fluke.

### 2c. How long skills take

- Amateur pedigree before turning pro: anything from a handful to ~400 fights. Examples: 75 (Owen), 97 (Paschall), ~396 to 398 (Lomachenko, one loss) [snippet]. Opponent quality matters more than the count [snippet, livestrong].
- Pros usually start in 4-rounders and build up to 6, 8, 10, 12 [mem]. Commentators said Loma needed "five or six fights" to learn the pro game [snippet].
- Expertise in general: the 10,000-hour figure comes from violinists; cricketers averaged ~7,300 hours; deliberate practice explains only about a third of skill variance in chess. **Feedback quality matters more than hours** [snippet, Ericsson + reanalyses]. No boxing-specific hours study found.
- Olympic medals predict pro titles: US gold and silver medalists were far more likely to win a world title, and got there faster (about 3x non-medalists overall). Bronze ≈ non-medalists [snippet: Rodriguez, Joslyn & Gruver, Sport Journal]. Cuban golds turned few greats: amateur skill transfers, but the pro game (pace, rough inside, 12 rounds) has to be learned [snippet, Boxing Scene].
- Rough timeline (est. from the above + [mem]): Novice → Intermediate ~1 to 2 years / 15 to 30 amateur bouts; Intermediate → Pro ~3 to 5 years / 50 to 150 amateur bouts or 10 to 20 pro fights; Pro → Worldclass: talent-gated, ~8 to 12 years in total, and most never get there.

### 2d. Camp, sparring, scouting

- Camp: 6 to 12 weeks, often 10, with two fitness peaks [snippet, Boxing Science]. Frampton ran 14 weeks [snippet].
- Sparring: about 2x per week, 6x3 min early in camp, up to 8 rounds per session at the peak, tapering in the last week [snippet, Frampton camp, Boxing News, Pacquiao camp].
- Sparring partners picked to look like the opponent: common [mem], no source found. (Ed: no sparring for now, so camp drills do this job.)
- Film study: fighters watch the opponent's fights for flaws, but **film only takes you so far**; the coach turns it into a plan in camp [snippet, DAZN]. Crawford watched "a couple" of Spence fights, let the coaches make the plan, and "preferred to make adjustments in the ring" [snippet, DAZN]. Pettaway studied Parker and found "little weakness in all that tape" [snippet].
- Trainers as scouts: Marquez + Beristain spent 4 months on one punch for one man [snippet].
- Take: scouting gives **tendencies** (what he likes, where he's weak), not **timing** (the live read). A plan from film works best against fixed habits and from a trainer who can teach it.

### 2e. Fighters who never adapt

- **One-plan punchers:** Wilder (Fury 3, Parker). Power fades as a threat once the other man has seen it [snippet].
- **Corner that can't adjust:** Canelo vs Mayweather, Wilder's corner in Fury 3 [snippet].
- **Lack of pro experience:** early Loma, out-roughed and tentative [snippet].
- **Habits under stress:** a tired or hurt fighter falls back on his deepest habit (`mind_vision.md` 7). A habit that got him beaten before comes back when he's hurt. Pacquiao's dropped guard "in kamikaze mode" [snippet].

## 3. Experience effects

### 3a. Ring generalship (veterans)

Bernard Hopkins as the model [snippet: Ring TV, Boxing Scene, Irish Times]:
- "Every aspect of ring generalship": feints, head movement, shoulder rolls, pivots, step-aways, spin moves, **economical punching**.
- **Slow start as bait:** "the bone on the string so the dog would follow it into a dark alley." Passive early, then work rate climbs once he has the range.
- **Imposes his fight:** draws opponents into fighting his way.
- **The cost:** vs Calzaghe, "one punch at a time, then clinch" didn't work against volume.

Veteran tricks, from trainer lore [mem]: lean on him and make him carry weight; tie up the lead hand; turn him on the ropes; work inside the clinch (hips, shoulders, Salido's game); waste nothing; steal the last 30 s; act fresh between rounds; take a breather when hurt (clinch, take a knee, buy time); hold-and-hit; know the referee.

### 3b. Composure under fire

`mind_vision.md` 6 and 8d covers composure. What experience adds:
- Has been hurt before and knows he recovers. A novice's first big shot sets off freeze/run; a vet clinches or takes a knee (est. + [mem]).
- Lower arousal with experience: novices show higher heart rate and anxiety in high-stress tasks; skill holds up either way [snippet, parachute study].
- Hard sparring = gradual stress exposure, the trainer's tool for this [snippet, mind_vision 6].

### 3c. How amateur pedigree shows

- **Shows up:** clean technique early, combos, defense, a high pace in short bursts, scoring awareness, comfort in the ring. Loma, Usyk, Inoue, Shakur (all elite amateurs) [mem].
- **Missing at first:** 12-round pacing, inside roughhousing, fouls, being hit by small gloves and heavier punchers, the weight game (Loma vs Salido) [snippet].
- **Pro-built (thin amateur record):** tougher, more rugged, slower on technique. Wilder started boxing at 20, Fury had ~35 amateur fights [mem]. Ruiz/Wilder types: power and heart before craft.

## 4. For the brain

Everything here is (est.): starting numbers for `tests/levels.js` (L0) to tune against. Tiers: **N**ovice, **I**ntermediate, **P**ro/Elite, **W**orldclass. Mastery `m` is 0 to 100 per skill: N 0 to 30, I 30 to 55, P 55 to 80, W 80 to 100. Effects interpolate on `m`; the table shows tier centers.

### 4a. Per-skill gaps

**Offense**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Jab force | .60 | .80 | .92 | 1.0 | Smith 2000 lead hand |
| Hand speed | 1.0 | 1.0 | 1.0 | 1.0 | Speed doesn't change with skill (wearable study): keep it a stat |

**Bad technique on an unowned skill** (Ed's rule: still tried, badly): use the N column for that skill plus a "nobody's buying it" check: a feint with `m<30` reads as a feint 70% of the time against `m>55` IQ.

**Upsets:** with every skill one tier down, aim for the underdog winning 15 to 20% (Ed). A knob check: elite-vs-novice force alone is 2x, so cap the combined tier-gap edge or N vs W ends at 0%. Real life agrees: novices don't beat world-class pros (est. ~0 to 1%).

### 4b. Learning between fights

**Growth (mastery XP)**
- Gain per fight per skill: `dm = base * use * success * teacher * (1 - m/cap)^1.5`. Base ~3 to 5 points for a skill he used a lot. Diminishing near the cap.
- **Losses teach more than wins,** but only about what beat him: a loss to a skill gives +50 to 100% XP on the matching **defensive** or **counter** skill (KO'd by the right hand → blocking/slipping vs right hands, clinch when hurt). (est., from Wlad/Pacquiao/AJ)
- **The after-KO shift:** a KO loss sets `caution += .1 to .2` (higher guard, fewer risks, more clinch) for 2 to 4 fights, then it fades halfway. Low composure + KO loss → `gunShy` instead (slower trigger, Wilder vs Parker). A Fury-type (high heart, power) may flip to more aggressive instead.
- **Teacher multiplier:** a good corner/trainer 1.5x, none 0.6x. The coach is the big lever ("a new trainer is usually the trigger").
- **Ceilings:** a talent cap per skill rolled at creation (most fighters top out in P; W needs talent ≥ ~85). Age: XP x1.2 under 24, x1.0 to 30, x.6 after 32; legs (footwork, slipping) start to drop after ~32 to 34, IQ skills keep growing (Hopkins).
- **Pace of growth (target):** N → I in ~8 to 15 fights, I → P in ~15 to 30 more, P → W only with talent, ~30+ more. Each skill grows at its own rate: a jab used every fight passes a body attack he never throws.
- **Fight experience curve:** the first 5 to 10 pro fights also add "pro game" knowledge (rough inside, 12-round pacing, fouls) on top of amateur mastery (Loma vs Salido).

**Opponent memory (`sheet.opp[id]`)**
- Keep after a fight: his top 3 habits from `F.mem` (lead, follow-up, defense), what hurt me, the result, the round of the stoppage.
- Decay: x.85 per fight in between (est.), floor .3: you never fully forget a man who stopped you.
- Rematch effect: start the fight with `F.mem` seeded from it (worth ~1 to 2 rounds of live reading at the same IQ), plus `ANT` ×1.1 vs his known lead. The man who lost gets an extra +1 adjustment pre-loaded (Bivol: "start earlier").
- Target check: rematch with no other changes → first winner ~70 to 80% (2b). If the loser's lesson flips it more than ~35%, the bonus is too big.
- Both men learn: the winner remembers too (Usyk's R4 switch). Don't give the bonus only to the loser.

**Style/stance familiarity (`sheet.know`)**
- Per style and stance 0 to 1. Each fight vs that style +.15 to .25 (diminishing); camp drill +.2 to .3 for one style.
- Unfamiliar (`know < .3`): reads at half speed for the first 3 to 4 rounds, the predicted-punch bonus halved, more caught by the main weapon (southpaw left).
- The data: both orthodox and southpaw pros win less often against southpaws (340 pros, 2014) [snippet]. Scarcity, not stance itself, is the edge, so familiarity is the right lever.

### 4c. Scouting knob (pre-fight film study)

`scout` 0 to 1 = film hours × trainer skill (the trainer turns film into a plan).

- **What it gives:** seeds `F.mem` with up to `scout * .5` of his real habit weights (lead punch, favorite follow-up, defense side, weak spots like a dropped hand after the jab), with noise `± (1 - scout) * .3`. It never gives timing: anticipation still has to be earned live, so scouting raises the *prior*, and live reading confirms or overrules it.
- **Cap:** full scouting is worth about 1 to 1.5 rounds of live reading for a P-level reader (est.). A W reader gains less (he reads live anyway; Crawford "preferred to adjust in the ring"). A Novice can't use it (`effect *= m_IQ/100`).
- **Stale film:** if the opponent changed (new trainer, a style switch after a loss) the seeded habits are wrong. Give a 20 to 30% chance per changed fighter that 1 seeded habit is flipped. The smart fighter drops a prior that keeps lying within ~1 round (same "trust" mechanic as feints, `mind_vision.md` 8c). The dumb one keeps waiting for a punch that never comes. Funny and real.
- **Plan drill (camp):** pick one weapon vs one habit (Marquez's counter right on the step-in). That counter gets +15 to 25% of the success the read gives, only vs that habit, for this fight. Costs camp time from other skills.
- **Fixed-habit fighters get exploited by scouting most:** scout value × (opponent habit strength). A low-IQ man with strong habits is the best film target. A W adapter is the worst.

### 4d. Commentary + corner hooks
- "He's telegraphing that right hand, you can see it coming from the parking lot." (N power shot)
- "Looks like he's watched the tape: he's slipping that jab before it's thrown." (scout prior working)
- "That's not the same guy who got stopped last time, tighter guard, he's learned." (post-KO caution)
- "Nobody told him this guy switches to southpaw." (stale film / low `know`)
- "First time he's been hit like that as a pro, let's see what he's made of." (N/I composure check)
