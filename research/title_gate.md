# How a man gets a title shot

Why this file exists: the game's gate is top 5 and it never produces a
title shot — 0 in 54 test careers (Claude's 48×30 + Stephie's 6×50). This
is how it works in the real world, from the sanctioning bodies' own
rulebooks (WBC and IBF read in full; WBO/WBA via official resolutions
quoting their rules) and documented fight history.

Headline: the real gate is **top 15, not top 5** — and even top 15 has
written exceptions. A top-5-only gate is far stricter than reality, and
that is most of why the career mode never produces a shot.

## The gate: how far down can a shot go?

**Top 15 is the usual floor, with exceptions.**

- **WBC Rule 3.3 (Qualified Challengers):** top 10 freely; 11–15 with
  majority Board of Governors approval; a champion or highly-rated boxer
  of another weight division; another WBC champion (Diamond, Silver,
  Emeritus, in Recess); a former world champion or other elite challenger;
  a champion of another WBC-recognized organization; or — with a 2/3
  Board vote under "special circumstances" — **a contender not otherwise
  listed, i.e. anyone.**
- **IBF:** optional (voluntary) defenses must be against "any contender
  rated in the top fifteen (15)" with Chairman/President approval. No
  written sub-top-15 exception found, but other organizations' champions
  can be designated "elite contenders" for unification bouts.
- **WBO:** between mandatories, the champion "may defend the title against
  any contender classified within the fifteen (15) best," with Committee
  approval.
- **WBA:** no explicit top-15 challenger rule found in the material
  reviewed — the Championships Committee has broad discretion. **Flag: do
  not assume a WBA top-15 floor.**

**Outside top 15, documented:**
- Andy Ruiz Jr. fought Joshua for the WBA/IBF/WBO titles (June 1, 2019)
  ranked **WBC #18 and unrated by the WBO** (WBA #12, IBF #14). The WBA
  moved him #12 → #5 the same day he signed.
- Manny Pacquiao fought for the WBC welterweight title (July 19, 2025)
  after four years out; the WBC first reinstated him at #5, then removed
  him from the rankings while still authorizing the fight under its rules
  for legendary former champions. WBC president Mauricio Sulaiman admitted
  ranking him #5 was "a mistake" but said the regulations allow legendary
  boxers who pass their exams to compete for the title.
- Amir Khan challenged for the WBC middleweight title in 2016 having
  never fought at middleweight.

## Mandatory challenger

A mandatory is the contender a body orders its champion to face by a
deadline; refusing (without an approved exception) means being stripped.
**Being ranked #1 does NOT automatically make a boxer the mandatory —
designation is a separate act by each body.**

| Org | How the mandatory is decided | How often the champ must face one |
|---|---|---|
| WBC | President + Board vote designates; by final elimination bout, designating a Qualified Challenger, or ordering a unification. "The highest rated boxer is never, solely by virtue of such position, the official mandatory challenger." | At least **once a year**, unless the WBC grants an exception. A new champion inherits the mandatory obligations of the champion he beat. |
| WBA | Championships Committee selection **or** an eliminatory bout. "Generally, the highest rated boxer, or regional champion rated in the top 5, shall qualify." | **9 months** (12 at heavyweight) from title acquisition if the champ was previously the official contender; **120 days** (180 at HW) if he was not. Unified 18 months, undisputed 21, super 24. |
| IBF | Championships Chairman designates the "leading available contender" ~60 days before the due date. Boxers contracted elsewhere, injured, ill, legally impeded or suspended count as unavailable; next in line is designated. | **Every 9 months** (heavyweight: first within 1 year, then every 9 months). Widely regarded as the strictest enforcer. |
| WBO | World Championship Committee selects "the highest classified available boxer." A classified boxer may also **petition in writing** for mandatory designation. | **9 months** (12 at heavyweight); a Super Champion may fulfill within **18 months**. No more than two mandatories in that period; the second not before 90 days after the first. |

**If the champion refuses:** the title is vacated/stripped. IBF: failing to
sign a purse-bid contract within 15 days → title vacated **and** removal
from the ratings for at least 6 months. Real cases: Usyk vacated the IBF
title (June 2024) rather than face mandatory Daniel Dubois; Hamzah Sheeraz
vacated the WBO super-middleweight title (Sept 2026) rather than proceed
with his mandatory against Zhanibek Alimkhanuly after the purse bid.

**If the challenger refuses:** WBC: mandatory status can be divested. IBF:
next in line is designated and the refusing challenger drops below #10 for
at least 6 months. WBO: the Committee selects the next available contender.

**Step-aside deals (flag: standard practice, NOT in any rulebook reviewed):**
a mandatory can be paid a "step-aside fee" to temporarily waive his shot
while the champion takes a bigger fight.

## Title eliminator

A bout between highly ranked contenders ordered by a body; the winner
becomes (or moves toward becoming) the mandatory.

- **WBC distinguishes eliminators from final eliminators.** Only the winner
  of a bout explicitly sanctioned as a **"final elimination"** becomes
  mandatory. The WBC may order a second final eliminator if the first
  result was controversial, and may revoke an eliminator winner's status
  for a controversial outcome, unsatisfactory performance, or inactivity.
  Eliminator purse bids split 50/50.
- **IBF:** the #1 and #2 positions are left **vacant** until two top-5
  leading available contenders ranked #10 or above fight a 12-round
  elimination bout; boxers in bouts shorter than 12 rounds cannot rank
  above #3. The Chairman may also order a **box-off** among the leading
  four available contenders ranked #10 or above. No extensions in
  elimination bouts; refusal → drop below #10 for 6+ months. Eliminator
  purse splits: #1 vs #2 = 50/50; #1 or #2 vs anyone else = 75/25;
  #3-or-below vs #3-or-below = 60/40 to the higher-ranked boxer.
- **WBA:** a mandatory "may be selected by the Championship Committee or
  through an eliminatory bout."
- **WBO:** the Committee may order eliminatory bouts; an interim title
  fight can also serve as the eliminator route.

An eliminator win does not guarantee a prompt shot: the mandatory's turn
can be delayed by unifications, exceptions and rotation between
organizations.

## Voluntary defense

A title defense against an opponent the champion (with his promoter)
chooses, rather than one the body orders — subject to the body's approval
and ranking eligibility.

- **WBC:** champions "shall propose voluntary defenses against Qualified
  Challengers" (top 10 freely; 11–15 with Board approval; plus the
  former-champion/other-champion exceptions). Champions are supposed to
  defend ~3 times a year (every 120 days), so voluntary defenses fill the
  gaps between mandatories.
- **IBF (called "optional defenses"):** against any top-15 contender with
  Chairman/President approval, and they must conclude at least 60 days
  before the mandatory due date.
- **WBO:** between mandatories, the champion may defend against any top-15
  contender with Committee approval.
- **WBA:** voluntary defenses fill the period before the 9-month mandatory
  deadline; the champion cannot fight a non-official-challenger within 60
  days after the mandatory period expires.

**How champions use them:** bigger-money or lower-risk fights, staying
active while a mandatory is unavailable, or buying time. A voluntary
defense against a top contender can also satisfy or extend a mandatory
obligation (WBC). If a champion loses a voluntary defense, the new champion
inherits the mandatory obligation.

## Purse bids

When a bout is ordered, the parties get a free-negotiation period — **30
days** at the WBC, IBF and WBA, **20 days** at the WBO. If no deal is
reached, the body calls a purse bid: promoters submit sealed bids for the
right to promote the fight; the highest bidder wins; the fight must be
staged within **90 days** (WBC, IBF).

**Splits (champion vs challenger, title bouts):**

| Org | Standard split | Vacant title |
|---|---|---|
| WBC | **70/30** of the 90% remaining after a **10% winner's bonus** is set aside off the top | 50/50 |
| WBA | **75/25** | Not verified |
| IBF | **65/35** vs a challenger ranked #1 or #2; **85/15** vs a challenger ranked #3 or below | 50/50 |
| WBO | **75/25** if the fight is in the champion's country or a neutral venue; **80/20** if in the challenger's country | Not verified |

Real examples: Usyk–Dubois (WBA, May 2023): K2 won with $8,057,000;
Usyk 75% ($6,042,750), Dubois 25% ($2,014,250). Wilder–Povetkin (WBC, Feb
2016): World of Boxing won with $7.15m; WBC held 10% ($715,000) as a
winner's bonus, balance split 70/30.

**If the champion refuses / the bid fails:**
- Champion refuses to sign or participate: WBC — forfeits the title (2/3
  Board vote to confirm). IBF — title vacated and fighter removed from
  ratings for at least 6 months. WBO — Committee may declare him
  unavailable and select the next contender.
- Challenger refuses: WBC — forfeits challenger status. IBF — removed
  from rankings for at least 6 months.
- No bids: WBC — extends the bid period ~15 days. IBF — a second purse
  bid is held; if still no bids, "the championship may be vacated"; for
  an eliminator with no bids, both boxers drop out of the top 10 for at
  least 6 months. WBO — the Committee may reduce the minimum bid by up to
  50% and rebid, or grant the champion up to 120 days for a voluntary
  fight.
- Winning promoter defaults: WBC — 10% deposit may be forfeited and
  distributed to the boxers; rights may go to the second-highest bidder.
  IBF — bid falls to the next-highest bidder; defaulting bidder barred
  from IBF purse bids for at least 6 months.

## How rankings move

**All four bodies use committees. None publishes a public points formula.**
"The WBA, WBC, IBF and WBO don't publish a public points formula. Their
rankings are set by committees that weigh title activity, eliminators,
regional belts, opponent quality and judgement calls."

- **WBC — committee, explicitly subjective.** Ratings Committee of
  chairmen/vice-chairmen from each continental federation; decisions
  "based upon consensus of the committee." The rules state plainly:
  "Ratings are and may be largely subjective." Monthly meeting (first
  Sunday, up to 7 hours); once a year the committee meets for three days
  with an open floor where anyone can argue a case. **Rates 40 fighters
  per division** (the other three rate 15).
- **WBA — committee, opinion-based.** Ratings Committee of at least 3
  members; ratings "represent the best opinion of the Association as to
  the relative qualification of the boxers."
- **IBF — committee applying written criteria; historically
  computer-assisted.** Ratings "must be solely based on win/loss records,
  level of competition, amateur achievement, activity, and a boxer's
  adherence to IBF/USBA Rules," following ABC guidelines. **Disagreement
  flagged:** one secondary source describes the IBF system as "a point
  system," but the IBF's published criteria describe committee judgment
  with no formula, and no points table is published.
- **WBO — Classification Committee applying ABC-based criteria.** Rankings
  are "guided by objective standards under the Muhammad Ali Boxing Reform
  Act and ABC" — "based on merit, not opinion" (WBO's own wording).

Other structural differences: the IBF leaves #1 and #2 vacant until an
elimination bout fills them. The WBO and IBF do not rate a boxer in more
than one division. The IBF removes boxers who contract to fight for other
world titles from its ratings.

### How far does one big win jump a fighter?

There is no fixed exchange rate — committees move fighters by judgment —
but documented jumps are large:

- **Agit Kabayel (WBC):** entered at **#30** (May 2017); one win over
  Derek Chisora (Nov 2017) "catapulted him directly into the world Top
  15" (15+ spots). Beating unbeaten Arslanbek Makhmudov (Dec 2023) moved
  him "immediately" to **#8**. Beating Zhilei Zhang (Feb 2025) made him
  interim champion.
- **Zhilei Zhang (WBO):** ranked **#13**, he beat WBO interim champion Joe
  Joyce (TKO6, April 15, 2023) and took the interim title — becoming the
  WBO mandatory challenger in a single fight.
- **Andy Ruiz Jr. (WBA):** moved from **#12 to #5** (7 spots) on the day
  he *signed* to fight Joshua — without fighting at all — showing rankings
  also move for administrative/commercial reasons.
- Regional titles move fighters too: Kabayel's European Union title put
  him at #30; his NABF title (via Makhmudov) put him at #8.

**Rule of thumb for the simulator:** a win over a top-10 opponent can
reasonably jump a fighter 5–15 spots or into the top 5; a win over a
champion/interim champion can confer a mandatory slot outright. Small wins
over unranked opponents move a fighter little or not at all.

### How far does a loss drop a fighter?

- **Deontay Wilder (WBC) — documented cascade:** WBC #1 for years → **#1
  to #8** after losing to Joseph Parker (Dec 2023); → **#14** after being
  knocked out by Zhilei Zhang (June 2024), eventually as low as **#15**.
  *The Ring* removed him from its top 10 entirely after the Zhang loss.
- **Losing to an unranked opponent is punished most harshly:** WBO rules —
  a ranked boxer who loses to an unranked opponent drops and the winner
  enters the rankings. WBA rules — the Committee may demote or remove a
  boxer for "loss to a non-rated boxer."
- **Losing a title fight does not necessarily mean a big drop** — a
  competitive loss to the champion often keeps a fighter in the top 5
  (e.g., Zhang stayed highly ranked after losing to Joseph Parker in March
  2024). **Flag:** no fixed "title-loss drop" number is published by any
  body; this is committee judgment.
- **Mandatory-position penalties are codified and severe:** an IBF
  challenger who refuses a mandatory or eliminator is dropped **below #10
  for at least 6 months**; an IBF boxer who fails to sign a purse-bid
  contract is **removed from the rankings for at least 6 months**.

### Does inactivity cost ranking spots?

Yes, at every body — but the thresholds differ:

| Org | Inactivity rule |
|---|---|
| WBC | "A rated boxer who does not compete in **9 months** will be subject to being lowered in the rankings." To maintain a top-15 rating, a boxer should have fought another rated boxer within **18 months**. Documented: Wilder slid **#10 → #13** between June and October 2025 purely on inactivity. |
| WBA | The Committee may demote or remove a boxer for "inactivity" — **no fixed period is stated in the rules reviewed.** (The 9-month figure found applies to WBA *Continental* champions' titles, not necessarily world rankings.) |
| IBF | To remain in the top 15, a boxer must "compete at least once every **twelve (12) months**" and within 6 lbs of his rated weight; otherwise he "will be lowered or removed from the ratings." |
| WBO | To stay ranked: compete at least once every **12 months** and face another top-rated boxer at least once every **18 months** (medical-verified injury exception). #1 and #2 must "defend [their] classification at least annually." |

Note the tension: inactivity rules are enforced unevenly — star fighters
have kept rankings through long layoffs (Wilder never left the WBC top 15
despite one win in 30 months; Pacquiao was ranked #5 after four years out).
Enforcement discretion is itself part of the system.

## Shortcuts

### The "name" fighter

A title shot off money/TV appeal before the ranking earns it. The
mechanisms: voluntary defenses (a champion picks a commercially attractive
opponent from the top 10/15 — or, at the WBC, a "former world champion or
other elite challenger" regardless of ranking); rankings adjusted to fit
the fight (Ruiz bumped #12 → #5 on signing day; Pacquiao inserted at #5
after four years out); WBC "Champion Emeritus" / special-bout rules (a
Champion Emeritus may request an immediate title shot; the WBC may sanction
a "special bout" as a title defense that takes precedence over a
mandatory). Promoter/network pressure is the underlying driver, and bodies
collect sanction fees (~3% of purses, capped) — **flag: the incentive
reading is interpretation, not a rulebook fact.**

### Interim and "regular" belts as a back door

- **Interim titles** are created when the full champion cannot defend in a
  reasonable time (injury, inactivity, unification commitments). The
  interim champion is positioned as next in line — typically the mandatory
  challenger for the full title. If the full champion vacates, is stripped,
  retires or moves divisions, the interim champion is usually **elevated
  to full champion without fighting.**
  - **IBF:** interim title bouts are between the mandatory challenger and
    the next highest-ranked available contender; an interim champion may
    hold the title for a maximum of **12 months**.
  - **WBA:** Rule C.28 — the President and Championships Committee "may
    elevate an interim champion to champion at their discretion."
  - **WBC:** the WBC has awarded interim titles to winners of final
    eliminators, signifying mandatory status; interim champions have been
    elevated on vacancy (Kabayel, June 2026).
  - **WBO:** interim champions are sanctioned under Section 14; the
    interim champion becomes the mandatory (Zhang, 2023).
- **WBA "Regular" titles:** when the WBA designates a "Super" champion (a
  champion holding multiple organizations' titles, or with 5+ defenses),
  it may fill a separate "Regular" title in the same division. The Regular
  champion is effectively the mandatory for the Super champion, and the
  WBA orders a "consolidation" bout between them. (Dubois won the Regular
  title in June 2022 and thereby became Usyk's mandatory.)
- **WBC "Franchise" champion (2019, created for Canelo Alvarez):** an
  honorary designation that lets an elite champion skip normal mandatory
  obligations while the WBC crowns another full champion — a shortcut
  *for the champion*, not the challenger.

### Moving up or down a weight class

- **Moving up is the classic shortcut.** A star moving up can be installed
  directly as a mandatory or handed a voluntary title shot in his first
  fight in the division: the bodies' rules allow champions of other
  divisions/orgs as qualified challengers (WBC Rule 3.3.4, 3.3.7), and the
  WBO grants its "Super Champions" mandatory status when they move up
  (Usyk, 2019). Canelo moved up two divisions to light heavyweight and won
  the WBO title in his divisional debut (Nov 2019); Amir Khan moved up two
  divisions for a middleweight title shot (May 2016); Usyk moved from
  cruiserweight to heavyweight and was installed as WBO mandatory.
- **Moving down** works the same way in principle; **flag: no specific
  documented "move down for a quick shot" case was verified** — the
  verified cases are all moves up.
- **Ranking consequence:** a boxer changing divisions is "not assured of a
  ranking in his new category" (WBA rules); the WBO and IBF rate a boxer
  in only one division at a time. In practice, elite movers are ranked
  immediately in the new division on reputation.

## What this means for the game

- **The gate should be top 15, not top 5.** A top-5-only gate is far
  stricter than reality — that is most of why no career ever produces a
  shot. Outside-top-15 shots should be rare but possible via the
  former-champion / other-org-champion / special-circumstances exceptions.
- **A mandatory clock would change the shape of careers.** Champions must
  face a mandatory every 9–12 months or be stripped; the mandatory is a
  designated man, not simply #1. That gives the player a second route to
  a shot besides grinding to #1: win an eliminator, hold an interim belt,
  or be designated.
- **Eliminators are the mid-tier route.** A #2–5 man who wins a final
  eliminator becomes mandatory without waiting for the #1 spot.
- **Voluntary defenses are the champ's choice between mandatories** —
  money fights, tune-ups, delay. If the champ loses one, the new champ
  inherits the mandatory obligation.
- **Interim and "regular" belts are real back doors.** Winning one
  typically makes the holder the mandatory, and interim champions are
  routinely elevated to full champion without a fight when the champion
  vacates.
- **Rankings move by judgment, with wide variance.** A win over a top-10
  opponent can jump a fighter 5–15 spots or into a mandatory slot; a loss
  can cost 7+ spots; losing to an unranked man is punished hardest;
  inactivity costs spots after 9–12 months. No body publishes a formula —
  the game should model movement as judgment-based, not a fixed exchange
  rate.
- **Purse bids are the fallback when negotiations fail** — 30 days to
  negotiate (20 at the WBO), sealed bids, fight within 90 days, splits
  from 85/15 (IBF vs #3+) to 50/50 (vacant). A champion who refuses is
  stripped.

## Gaps — what we could not verify

- WBA top-15 floor for challengers: no explicit rule found in the material
  reviewed. Do not assume one.
- WBA inactivity period for ranked contenders: the rules allow
  demotion/removal for "inactivity" but state no fixed period. The 9-month
  figure found applies to WBA *Continental* champions' titles, not
  necessarily world rankings.
- WBA and WBO vacant-title purse-bid splits: not verified (WBC and IBF
  are 50/50).
- IBF "points system": one secondary source claims the IBF uses a point
  system; the IBF's published criteria describe committee judgment with no
  formula, and no points table is published.
- Fixed ranking-move numbers: no body publishes how many spots a win or
  loss is worth; all movement figures above are documented examples, not
  formulas.
- Move-down shortcut: no verified case of a fighter moving *down* a weight
  class specifically for a quicker title shot; all verified weight-move
  cases are moves up.
- Step-aside fees: established practice, widely reported, but not codified
  in any rulebook reviewed.
- WBO regulations full text: the WBO regulations PDF had garbled text
  extraction; WBO rules here are cited from official WBO resolutions and
  purse-bid orders that quote the regulations, plus the WBO's own "WBO
  101" posts — reliable but second-order relative to the rulebook itself.
