## Q1. What it stops
- Mostly the other man's LEAD-hand weapons: his jab and lead hook. The posted hand covers the jab lane before the punch starts ("traffic") or pins his glove. [M: Evolve, eBoxing, Skillr]
- Davis vs Garcia is the clean case: Garcia landed 3 of 21 jabs in R1, 0 of 8 in R2, then never more than 3 a round. Davis also threw few jabs himself, which the narrator calls a sign of an active long guard. [S eBoxing]
- Straights in general get parried, trapped or framed by the extended arm. [M: Wikipedia, Evolve]
- Hooks must travel around the arms, so they come slower; with a reach edge, hooks and uppercuts are hard to land. That is the double (mummy) version. [S Wikipedia]
- It blinds: a glove in the face hides the next shot (jab, then right behind it). [S Skillr]
- It stops forward momentum: a framed arm halts a man walking in with power shots. [S Evolve]
- It does NOT reliably stop the rear straight. Evolve says rear-side punches stay available to the opponent once his lead hand is controlled. Skillr says the rear hand is the weak link: long-guard men lean on the lead hand and mistime rear-hand parries. [M, 2 sources] This contradicts "jams the rear straight".
- Window off your own jab: no source says it shrinks the counter window directly. The mechanism I can support: the posted hand is already out (a measure/probe, no step, little weight), so he is not committed and not lunging. Skillr says measuring is low-commitment and "best used at long range". [S, inference]
- It can also bait. Davis kept a light touch that looked like a trap, left Garcia the hook, ducked it and countered. That produced the knockdown and the liver KO. [S eBoxing; Bloody Elbow has a similar roll-under-the-hook version, sources disagree on the exact punch] So the long guard pays off as a counter platform vs hooks.

## Q1. What beats it (ranked)
Skillr's counter clip is the only dedicated answer list. Ranking is mine, from how the sources frame it.
1. **Feints** (Skillr counter list first; 1 source says first). They make the lead arm tire, since holding it out costs more energy than a normal guard. They force the man to step back or switch to Philly or high guard, which breaks the long guard. A body feint drops his lead hand. [S Skillr; the fatigue point echoed in Skillr 1/2]
2. **Guard manipulation** (peel the lead hand down, outside parry then lead hook, raise it). Then the overhand or cross over the top, or a jab over it in opposite stance. [S Skillr] Wikipedia/Evolve in MMA: grab the outstretched arms to start a clinch. [S]
3. **Body shots on the lead side** (open side). Level-change feint then jab or cross to the body; jabs to the body repeat well against it and set up the head. [M: Skillr, Wikipedia, Evolve all list "lead side exposed"; only Skillr gives the how]
4. **Rear-hand side attack**: get the head off the centerline (inside/outside slip or roll) and hit the side of the rear hand, which parries badly. [S Skillr]
5. **Walking in under it with lateral movement**: opponents can measure the reach and close with lateral movement. [S Wikipedia] Tank used his own hand traps to step and slip in on the taller man. [S eBoxing]
6. **Uppercuts and straights that get past the arm** (little passive cover for these). [M: Wikipedia x2 pages, Evolve]
7. **Lead hook / uppercut**: weak answer. The lead arm must be pulled back first, so the man throws them slower and telegraphed. A hook over the top is not a strong counter-source. [M: Wikipedia, Evolve]
8. **Low lead hand or Crab-style** fighters beat the mummy form (the arms are not where they expect). [S Wikipedia]
- Counters over the arm (Usyk v Joshua): the repo notes (breakdowns.md 51-52) say Joshua picked at Usyk's lead hand, Usyk showed the hand to bait him. For Fury, Fight Library says Usyk beat the long guard by outside-foot position, stepping on the jab and looping the left back as Fury left; it never names a single "answer". [S]
- Main answer: no coach names ONE. The Skillr list order (feint first, then manipulation, then body) is the closest to an answer. I'd say "make him tired and move his hand, then hit the open body". [?]
- Klitschko v Fury: a body-work plan was urged but rarely used against the posted jab; Fury beat the long jab with feints, movement, switching and clinching. [S, coverage] Not a long-guard source as such.

## Q1. What it costs
- Arm fatigue: holding the arm out is more energy demanding than a normal guard; constant repositioning from feints makes it worse. [S Skillr]
- Body exposed on the lead side. [M, 5 sources]
- Out of position to punch with power while the hand is away (the measuring hand). [S Skillr 1/2] Wikipedia also says long guards give less punch power than a bent-arm guard. [S]
- Reaching leaves a hole in the guard and wide open on that side if you have not trained the long-guard defenses. [S Skillr 1/2]
- Lead hook/uppercut slow and telegraphed, as above. [M]
- Little passive defense against straights or uppercuts that pass the arm. [M]
- The man reading you gets reactive: long-guard men step back or readjust to feints. [S Skillr]
- Davis's long guard cost him jab volume of his own. [S eBoxing] "You trade lead-hand weapons for tools."

## Q2. Who uses it (by style)
- **Tall out-boxers / jab-and-grab**: Wladimir Klitschko, Fury, Bivol named as taller men who jab from range behind it. [S Dynamic Striking snippet via search, plus Evolve-adjacent] Fury used it vs Usyk; reach made it work, paired with the shoulder roll and pawing at Usyk's lead hand, clinching to stop his lateral move. [S Fight Library]
- **Southpaw long guards**: Nakatani tries to take lead-hand control early so opponents cannot jab; leads with the left cross not the jab. [S Fight Library] His weakness: when Cuellar got the outside foot, his jab lacked power. size.md 312 covers Tarver v Jones.
- **Short counter-puncher**: Tank Davis from southpaw vs taller Garcia. Not for jabbing: it denies the jab, steps in behind hand traps, throws a safe cross, and baits the hook for a counter. [S eBoxing, plus Dynamic Striking snippet] This is the main example that it is not only a tall man's tool.
- **Shorter men "intermittently"**: Wikipedia says shorter fighters often use it in moments. [S]
- **Mummy / double long guard**: Foreman, Sandy Saddler, Miguel Canto (Wikipedia). Foreman as a pressure fighter with arms out is a stretch; I have not checked clips. [S, ?]
- **Gloves-in-face probers**: Bivol keeps a pawing jab into Canelo's high guard and used "traffic" per Skillr examples. [S]
- **Joshua**: proactive long guard, picks at the lead hand (breakdowns.md). Usyk read it.
- Not found in any source: Mayweather, Lomachenko, Canelo, Inoue, Hearns, Spinks, Wilder, Kovalev, Archie Moore. Canelo and Mayweather appear as targets or lead-hand users in other repo notes, not as long-guard users. Do not name them as users. [?]
- Tall man's tool? Mostly yes, but Davis shows a short counter-puncher can use it with traps and a baited hook. Out-boxer = defensive range tool; counter-puncher = a trap that draws one punch.

## Q2. How common
- Wikipedia calls it among the oldest guards, "still used"; taller men use it to keep range, shorter men in moments. [S] Skillr's lead-hand series says "all your favorite fighters" use controls like traffic, measure, post, and that these cost little. [S]
- My read: the posted hand as a MOMENT (measure, traffic, pin, post) is common at the top; the full sustained long guard is a specialist tool (Fury vs Usyk, Davis vs Garcia). [?]
- No stat on how often. Do not treat it as a rare exotic style.

## For Last Bell
- **Jams the rear straight over the jab**: the sources disagree. They say it stops his JAB and lead hook, and the rear hand is the hole. Suggest the jam acts on his jab (high), his lead hook (small), and the rear straight only a little. Keep a rear-hand opening: a man who goes off the centerline and attacks the rear-hand side lands more.
- **Shrinks the counter window**: weakly supported. Better framing: it cuts HIS jab output hard (Davis: 3/21, then 0/8), and gives the posting man a counter off his hook.
- **Gas cost**: supported. Fatigue is the stated reason feints beat it; add a bigger cost when the other man feints or hand-fights.
- **Lead-side body open**: supported by every source. Keep it and make a body feint (level change) the best answer.
- **Walk-in under it gets a free beat**: supported (lateral movement, head off the line, step and slip behind a trap).
- **Hook over the top small edge**: roughly right. Sources say lead hooks are slow from it; the overhand or cross over the top after a peel is the better answer, and an uppercut gets past the arm. Maybe make "peel the lead hand, then straight over" the edge, not the hook.
- **Users**: outboxer .8 fine (tall men, Klitschko/Fury/Bivol). Counter .2 may be low: Davis is the headline example and uses it as a trap that baits one punch; give counter a different effect (draws the hook) not just range. Boxer-puncher .25 and slugger/swarmer 0 have no source either way. Foreman is the only pressure example and it is thin [?].
- **Missing mechanic**: feints that make the posting man step back or switch guard. Not in the design.
