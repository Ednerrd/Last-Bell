# Batch 1 (Oct 20)

## 1. DelCardo Boxing: "5 fight adjustments from Usyk vs Fury" (Usyk-Fury I, round by round)
**The plans going in.** Fury wants to be chased: outbox you with the jab going backward, punch you as you step in, counter you over-extended. The more you chase, the better he does. Usyk wants to live in your face at a high pace, make you miss, punch your openings, and make you pay when you shell up or slow down; someone you can't hit who never leaves drains you, and he cashes it in late.
1. **R1-2, Usyk: punch his openings.** High pace, the open-stance lead-hand battle: every time Fury's arm leaves his body, Usyk goes around it and hits. Touch high so he guards high, then the body. Fury's lead hand, meant to keep the space, is the opening when he's on the ropes and can't step back.
2. **R2, Fury: more space, punch as he steps in.** He gives up the lead-hand game, keeps the hand home, moves away without losing eye contact, feints to hold the gap, and beats Usyk to the punch on the step-in. Coach's caveat: it only works with the distance read *and* the will to let the hands go; a gun-shy man who freezes just gets walked down.
3. **R3, Usyk: mix up the tempo.** His high output was playing into Fury's game (chasing makes you predictable). He keeps the pressure but changes *when* he attacks, lets openings go, keeps his space so Fury lands less; Fury has to stay alert, burns punches and gas keeping him off, and the moment Fury relaxes or resets, Usyk doubles the straight left (lean to the side, head off the line, double it).
4. **R4-7, Fury: bring the fight to him.** Against an unpredictable man, waiting for the counter stops working, so Fury takes the lead: more feints, the right uppercut, starts the exchange and leaves before Usyk answers, makes sure he's the last man punching, mixes front foot and back foot. "Accelerating the pace is not about more volume, it's dictating when exchanges start and end."
5. **R8-9, Usyk: punch before he resets.** Fury slows down (the work of keeping Usyk off, the damage); his feints stop, his shots lose snap, he backs up without feinting or punching, tries a lone right from too far. Usyk punches with him or right after he finishes, before he resets: that's the R9 knockdown. Caveat: it takes the quicker man vs a tiring or relaxing one; a slow man waiting for the other to finish finds him already gone.
- **R10-12: layering 3 + 5,** and *not* pushing the hurt man harder. Fury is still at his best when pressured, even hurt, so Usyk keeps the intensity low, stays unpredictable, and commits long combinations only when Fury opens up (often right after Fury's own attack): bank the rounds, take away Fury's one way back.

### For Last Bell (ideas, not built)
1. **Adjustments answer each other** (the core of this clip): each side's fix beats the other's last fix, round by round. A between-round `adjust` reads *why* he lost the round (countered on the way in / out-landed at range / gassed) and picks the matching answer: getting countered on entry -> change the tempo (attack less often, at odd times); his counter isn't landing -> lead himself; he's tired -> punch right after his punches.
2. **Tempo as a plan knob, separate from output** (adjustments 3 and 4): when he attacks vs how much he throws. Pick it up / Slow down are volume today.
3. **"Punch before he resets"** on a tired man: a counter window that opens with the other man's gas (his `tired` look) and the counterer's speed. Ties to the eyes (gas) and the counter timing.
4. **Don't push a hurt man whose game is fighting off pressure** (R10-12): a smart World-class leader keeps it low-risk rather than hunting. Ties to `HUNTS` and the finisher's choice: hunt by the other man's style, not only his hurt.
5. **Gun-shy kills the step-in counter** (adjustment 2 caveat): the counter plan needs the will to punch, not just the read (emotion `fear` lowering output undoes it).

## 2. Teddy Atlas: "How Bivol beat Canelo" (THE FIGHT, his breakdown)
- **When you knew it was over:** Bivol left his own fight and went inside, Canelo's ground, and *still* won those rounds. Winning on the other man's ground is the sign of a long night.
- **Bivol controlled the jab and the range** and put punches together. Bivol's trainer (with him since the amateurs) had the better plan than Reynoso.
- **Canelo's mistake: looking only for power.** Power you don't land puts you behind on the cards, and power needs a table set: the jab. Against a man with good legs who wants the outside, bring a jab to at least stabilize him there; Canelo didn't.
- **The rope show-off:** Canelo goes to the ropes and goes defensive for no reason but to show the defense: slipping, making you miss, **nothing coming back**. Atlas's plan said steal rounds right there: with no counter to deter him, throw more, put them together; "he can't slip them all". Bivol did, and landed.
- **Riding punches** (Duran's trick: turn with the right hand to take the sting out) cost Canelo with the judges: it made him look caught clean even when he wasn't. "A flashlight on himself." Better a clean slip that everyone sees miss, in position to counter.

### For Last Bell (ideas, not built)
6. **The punch-back test:** a man who defends without countering invites volume. The attacker reads "nothing coming back" (counters seen per defense, opponent memory) and throws longer combos; a defensive man must answer now and then or get piled on. (Same idea as the hurt man's "a punch now and then so he knows you're still there", `hurt.md` 1.)
7. **Power-only plans lose on the cards** (Canelo): a plan that skips the jab lands less and sets up nothing; the AI corner's call against a mover is "bring the jab".
8. **Rolling with punches looks worse to the judges:** a defense that takes the sting out but reads as clean (`judgeScore`) vs a slip that reads as a miss. Small, a judging-taste detail.
9. **Winning on his ground** as a sign for the corner: when the plan's ground is lost and the round still goes your way, the gap is real (between-round confidence).

## 3. The Modern Martial Artist: "How Usyk beat Joshua" (Usyk-Joshua I, tactics)
More a style study than a round-by-round fix; what made it work against a bigger man:
- **Always off the centre line:** feet and head moving together, level changes that also threaten the body and the power hand; small economical steps for exact angles and distance on the fly (Loma's coach).
- **The double jab:** the first (or a jab feint) draws a reaction, the second takes it. A feint doesn't need to look like a real punch: eyes follow quick movement, and after rounds of pressure that instinct wins.
- **The pendulum step:** the lead foot in and out at the edge of range dulls the reaction to the real one; step back from his punch and follow it back in, or step in to draw it and step back to counter (Holyfield).
- **Southpaw foot position:** outside foot for the straight left down the middle and to blunt his right; Usyk's own trick: foot outside, head slipped inside, to beat Joshua's jab with his own and still follow with the cross. A crossed-over corkscrew lead from the same spot.
- **Late, the pressure goes up:** 5-10 punch runs, doubling the same hand mid-combination to break the left-right defensive pattern.
- **Order:** wear him down first (movement, off-rhythm jabs, feints), *then* ramp it up.

### For Last Bell (ideas, not built)
10. **Same-hand doubles break the defense's rhythm:** a man defending left-right expects alternation; a double on one side (jab-jab, left-left mid-combo) gets through more. Check how `pickCombo` and the defender's read handle repeats.
11. **Feints wear the eyes down over rounds:** buying feints gets more likely as the fight goes on and the pressure piles up (fatigue + fear), not flat through the fight.
12. **Wear-then-ramp** as a World-class plan shape: low-risk early rounds to read and tire, a jump in volume late. Ties to the round-by-round plan and the drama numbers (late lead changes).
